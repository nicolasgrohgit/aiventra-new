# Incorporation options for Odys.ai

Founder-research, written pre-filing. No code changes accompany this document — it exists to
settle *where and how* to incorporate before any lawyer/accountant gets engaged. Companion in
spirit to `docs/monetization-strategy.md`: reasoned, sourced, and it ends by telling you what
it doesn't cover.

## The question this answers

The founder wants to incorporate **quickly, cheaply, with minimal money up front**, while
still getting a *real* company: one that can hold IP, sign contracts, open a business bank
account, take investment later, and — critically — formally register two or more named
co-founders against specific, documented equity percentages (a real cap table, not a
handshake). The starting hypothesis is Estonia, via e-Residency and an OÜ (osaühing, roughly
"private limited company"). This doc confirms that's the right mental model, prices it out in
real numbers, and checks it against three other EU jurisdictions with startup-formation
reputations: Lithuania, the Netherlands, and Ireland.

**Why these three as the comparison set, not others:** Lithuania because it directly competes
with Estonia for the same "digital-first, low-cost EU micro-company" niche and is the most
common answer when people say "Estonia's getting expensive/crowded, what else." The
Netherlands because it's the highest-credibility jurisdiction for **actually raising VC money
later** (most EU/US VCs are comfortable with a Dutch BV; convertible notes and SAFE-equivalents
are well understood there), which matters given the founder mentioned "take investment later."
Ireland because it's the default answer for founders wanting eventual US-investor and
Delaware-adjacent comfort inside the EU (English common-law-influenced company law, and it's
where a lot of US tech has EU subsidiaries) — it's included specifically as the "looks more
familiar to a future US investor" data point, even though it's the worst fit on cost/speed.
Non-EU jurisdictions (UK, Delaware) are out of scope per the founder's ask and are noted only
in "what this doesn't cover."

## Recommendation: Estonia OÜ, via the notary/proxy route rather than waiting on the e-Residency card

**Estonia is genuinely the right call for this founder's stated priorities** — cheapest
realistic all-in cost of the four (~€585–815 to form, ~€1,000–1,100/yr to maintain), no
resident-director requirement, no notary bottleneck for the common case (two private-person
founders), and a corporate tax structure that's unusually good for an early-stage company
specifically: **0% corporate income tax on profits that are retained or reinvested**, tax only
triggers at distribution (22/78 on the gross dividend, e.g. paying out €78,000 costs the
company €100,000 total), and that 22/78 rate survived a proposed 2026 hike to 24% when
Estonia's parliament cancelled it in December 2025.⁹ For a pre-revenue app that isn't
distributing profits any time soon, that 0%-until-distributed structure is close to free money
relative to Lithuania, the Netherlands, or Ireland, none of which offer anything equivalent.

The one correction to the founder's likely mental model: **don't wait for the e-Residency card
if speed matters.** The standard pitch ("e-Residency → register your company") implies you
need the card first, but the card itself typically takes 4–8 weeks to arrive after
application¹⁰ — that's the single biggest time cost in the entire Estonia path, and it's
avoidable. Estonian formation agents (Enty, 1Office, Thompson&Stein, and others) now offer a
**notarised power-of-attorney route**: you sign a POA via a video call with a licensed notary
(no travel), the agent registers the OÜ on your behalf through an Estonian notary instead of
the e-Business Register self-service portal, and the whole thing closes in as little as 2–4
business days.¹¹ You end up with the identical OÜ — same registry, same legal status — just
without the multi-week wait. Get the e-Residency card in parallel afterward if you want the
convenience of self-service digital signing for day-to-day contracts and filings later; it's
no longer the critical path to forming the company.

**Netherlands and Lithuania are the honest runners-up, for different reasons.** If "take
investment later" is weighted more heavily than "cheapest now," the Dutch BV is worth
revisiting once there's a real term sheet — it's the jurisdiction most VCs (EU and US) find
least friction in — but its notary requirement makes it slower and pricier to *start*, which
cuts against "quickly and cheaply" today. Lithuania is a legitimate near-tie with Estonia on
speed and simplicity, and doesn't ask you to think about e-Residency at all, but its €1,000
statutory minimum share capital (vs. Estonia's €0.01) and lack of an equivalent 0%-on-retained-
profits tax regime make it strictly worse for this specific founder's stated cost priority.
Ireland is the weakest fit for a founder not already based in the EEA — the resident-director-
or-bond requirement is a real, recurring cost (€1,500–2,000 every two years) that the other
three don't impose at all.

## How co-founder equity actually gets formalized in an Estonian OÜ

This is the part that most "how to get e-Residency" content glosses over, so it's worth being
concrete:

- **Share capital is nominal, not a real fundraising mechanism.** Since the February 2023
  reform, an OÜ has no fixed minimum share capital — a share can be worth as little as €0.01,
  and the reform also removed the old option to defer paying it in.¹² For two founders, this
  in practice means picking a small, round total (e.g. €100 total share capital, split €50/€50
  for a 50/50 split, or €60/€40 for 60/40) and actually contributing it — into the company's
  bank account — at incorporation. This is the number that gets registered, and it's what
  directly sets the ownership percentage: if Founder A holds shares nominally worth €60 and
  Founder B €40 out of €100 total, that's a real, registered 60/40 cap table, full stop. There
  is no separate "equity certificate" process the way there might be with a US C-Corp — the
  share register **is** the cap table.
- **This happens as part of incorporation itself, not as a bolt-on step.** When you (or your
  formation agent) file to establish the OÜ, you name every founder as a shareholder with a
  specific share value at that moment — you cannot register an OÜ with unallocated or "TBD"
  equity. If a third co-founder or an investor joins later, that's a separate share-capital
  increase / share-transfer filing against the existing registry entry, not a re-incorporation.
- **A shareholders'/founders' agreement is a genuinely separate document, and you should still
  write one even though the registry doesn't require it.** The share register tells you *what*
  the ownership split is; it says nothing about vesting, what happens if a founder leaves,
  decision-making thresholds, or transfer restrictions. Startup Estonia and the Estonian
  Private Equity & Venture Capital Association (EstVCA) publish free, lawyer-drafted model
  documents for exactly this — a Founders' Agreement template covering role definition, equity
  split rationale, and (critically) **reverse vesting**: the standard pattern cited is a
  4-year schedule with a 1-year cliff (25% vests at the 1-year mark, the remaining 75% vests
  monthly over the following 3 years), so that a founder who leaves early forfeits unvested
  shares back to the company/other founders rather than walking away with a full stake day
  one.¹³ This is not automatic — you have to actually adopt reverse vesting in that agreement;
  Estonian company law doesn't impose it by default. For two co-founders, get this signed
  alongside (or immediately after) incorporation, before any shares vest meaningfully — it's
  the single highest-leverage cheap-legal-document a two-founder startup can execute early.
- **Bottom line for "real cap table, not a handshake":** an Estonian OÜ genuinely delivers
  this. The registered share split is a public record in the Estonian e-Business Register
  (viewable by anyone, including future investors doing diligence), which is a stronger,
  more "real" signal than an informal side letter — but pair it with the founders' agreement
  above, because the registry alone won't protect either founder if one leaves in month three.

## What this means practically for a founder not physically in Estonia

**e-Residency is a digital-ID product for running the company, not an immigration or tax
product — and it's easy to over-read what it does.** It does not grant Estonian or EU
citizenship, a visa, tax residency, or the right to live or work in Estonia or anywhere else
in the EU. All it gives you is a digital identity that lets you register a company, sign
documents, and file with the Estonian tax authority online, from wherever you actually live.
Your personal tax residency stays exactly where it already was.¹⁵ This matters directly for
the founder's plan: incorporating the OÜ does not change where *you*, personally, owe income
tax — it only changes where the *company* is domiciled.

**Banking is the real friction point, and it's worth planning for up front rather than being
surprised by it.** Estonia's traditional banks have tightened up considerably on e-resident
accounts in the last few years — LHV, the most e-resident-friendly of them, still requires a
€300 non-refundable application fee, an in-person trip to Tallinn to open the account, and
evidence of genuine Estonian business substance (not just a registered address), plus ~€30/mo
for non-resident businesses.¹⁶ In practice, most e-resident-founded companies today don't try
to fight that: they run day-to-day operations on **Wise Business** (fully remote onboarding, a
real EU IBAN with SEPA, ~€50 one-off setup, no monthly fee, FX at close to mid-market
rates)¹⁴, and either skip the traditional Estonian bank account entirely or add LHV later
specifically for the EE-domiciled IBAN if a partner or investor insists on one. Wise is a
regulated EU payment institution, not a deposit-insured bank, which is a real distinction (no
deposit guarantee scheme) but is the accepted norm for this company type — it's explicitly
called out as an allowed alternative to a traditional bank account for OÜs in this position.¹⁶

**Everything else genuinely works remotely.** Company formation (via the notary/proxy route),
signing the founders' agreement, filing the annual report, VAT registration if/when needed —
all of it can be done without either founder setting foot in Estonia, which is the whole point
of the jurisdiction for a founder in this position.

## Addendum: reframed with time off the table, and the founder confirmed French

The founder followed up with a materially different question: if speed no longer matters, is
Estonia still cheaper, and given they're personally **French tax-resident**, do France, Spain,
or Andorra beat it? This changes the answer — being French isn't a neutral fact here, it's the
single biggest variable.

### The France-resident-specific factor the original analysis didn't need to weigh: Article 123 bis

A French tax resident who holds **≥10% of a foreign company subject to a "privileged tax
regime"** (broadly: taxed at least 40% below what French tax would have been) can be taxed in
France on that company's profits *as if distributed, even when nothing is actually paid out* —
this is Article 123 bis of the French tax code, the individual-level counterpart to the
Article 209 B corporate CFC rule already cited above.¹⁸ Estonia's headline 0%-on-retained-
profits regime is exactly the kind of thing this rule targets on its face.

**The decisive limitation, and why it likely doesn't bite here — but "likely" is doing real
work:** Article 123 bis only applies when the foreign entity's assets are **mainly composed of
financial assets** (securities, receivables, deposits, current accounts) — it's aimed at
passive "cash box" holding companies, not an actual operating business.¹⁸ An Estonian OÜ
running Odys.ai as a real product with real users is an operating company, not a cash box, so
the rule most likely doesn't apply to it in substance. But "mostly cash sitting in the company
account because it hasn't been reinvested yet" is exactly the fact pattern that could blur that
line for an early, thin-operations startup — this is a judgment call on the specific facts, not
something this document can settle, and it's precisely the kind of question worth a French
tax lawyer's five minutes before leaning on Estonia's 0% rate as a real advantage *for a French
founder specifically*. (Article 209 B, the corporate version, wouldn't apply directly here
either way — it requires a French *company* to hold >50% of the foreign entity, not a French
*individual*.)

### France itself, priced out

If the founder stays French tax-resident regardless of where the company sits, incorporating
**in France** (a SASU for one founder, or a SAS the moment there's a second) removes the
question entirely — there's no foreign entity for Article 123 bis to reach, no CFC exposure, no
Form 2047/3916 foreign-account reporting, no cross-border legal opinion needed.

- **Cost: the cheapest option of the four, not a close call.** DIY formation (self-filed
  statutes, no lawyer) runs **~€250–400 all-in**; the hard mandatory costs alone (legal notice
  publication, beneficial-owner declaration, registration) are ~€180–200. Even engaging a
  lawyer to draft the statutes properly tops out around €2,200–2,500 — still in the same band
  as Spain's *minimum*, for a jurisdiction with zero cross-border complexity on top.¹⁹
- **Minimum capital: €1**, same nominal-capital logic as Estonia — split it however the
  founders agree (€1 split €0.60/€0.40 is legally valid, though a slightly larger round number
  like €1,000 is more typical practice for credibility with banks/partners) and that's the
  registered cap table.
- **Corporate tax: 25% standard, but a real 15% reduced rate on the first €42,500 of profit**
  for small companies (turnover under €10M, ≥75% held by individuals — a two-founder startup
  qualifies easily).²⁰ This isn't as aggressive as Estonia's 0%-while-retained structure, but
  it's a genuinely competitive small-company rate, and — unlike Estonia for this specific
  founder — it comes with **no Article 123 bis question to even ask**.
- **Multi-founder equity: the standard case, not an edge case.** A SAS's articles of
  association (statuts) set each founder's share count directly at incorporation, exactly like
  Estonia's share register — France's SAS is in fact the *default* vehicle French startups use
  for this precise reason (flexible governance, easy multi-founder cap tables, the form every
  French startup lawyer and formation platform is built around). A separate pacte d'associés
  (shareholders' agreement) is the standard place to put reverse vesting, leaver clauses, and
  transfer restrictions — same structure as the founders'-agreement recommendation above for
  Estonia, just under a French name and with a deeper bench of local template availability.

### Spain and Andorra: neither is a better fit than France for this founder

- **Spain (SL): worse on every axis that matters here.** €1,500–3,500 to form (vs. France's
  ~€250–400), €3,000–8,000/yr ongoing compliance (vs. France's comparable or lower cost, and
  Estonia's ~€1,200–1,500/yr), and — critically — **the founder gets none of France's
  home-turf simplicity, because Spain isn't their home jurisdiction either.** A French resident
  incorporating in Spain still faces a foreign-entity question (a different country's version
  of "is this actually a low-tax regime relative to my home country," though Spain's ~25%
  standard CIT is not obviously privileged relative to France's, unlike Estonia's 0%). There is
  no scenario in this comparison where Spain beats both Estonia and France on cost — it only
  makes sense if the founder were actually relocating to Spain personally.
- **Andorra (SL): the most expensive of all four to form, and its real tax benefit isn't
  actually about where the company is incorporated.** €4,500–9,000 formation cost, €3,000
  minimum capital (fully paid up, vs. France's €1), a mandatory local registered office and
  Andorran bank account, and a foreign-investment-authorisation process that takes 2–3 months
  even with time off the table as a constraint.²¹ More importantly: Andorra's famous low-tax
  reputation (10% flat income tax) is a **personal tax-residency benefit**, not a
  company-formation one — to actually access it, the founder would need to become an Andorran
  *tax resident themselves* (which carries real physical-presence requirements, commonly cited
  around 90+ days/year, a genuine personal relocation decision), not just register a company
  there. Registering an Andorran SL while remaining a French tax resident gets the founder the
  worst of both: the highest formation cost of the four options, *plus* still being taxed in
  France on their personal income and very possibly still facing an Article 123 bis-style
  question on the company's retained profits, without any of the personal 10%-flat-tax benefit
  that makes Andorra interesting in the first place.

### Direct answer

**With time off the table, France is very plausibly the actual cheapest, simplest option for
this specific founder — not Estonia — precisely because they're French.** Estonia's 0%-on-
retained-profits structure is real and would still be the better *pure company-tax-rate* play
for a founder with no home-country CFC-adjacent exposure to it, but for a French tax resident
specifically, that advantage is muddied by Article 123 bis in a way this document can flag but
not resolve, while France offers a lower formation cost, a genuinely competitive 15% small-
company rate, and zero cross-border complexity. Spain and Andorra don't beat either option on
this founder's stated priorities. **The concrete next step, cheaply**: a single paid
consultation with a French tax lawyer or *expert-comptable* (routine, inexpensive relative to
what's at stake) to get a real answer on whether Article 123 bis would actually apply to an
Estonian OÜ running an active SaaS business — that one conversation resolves the actual open
question here, which is a legal-facts question, not a cost-comparison one.

Company formation, share-capital structuring, and founders' agreements have real, binding
legal consequences, and cross-border ownership can trigger obligations in a founder's home
country that this document does not and cannot assess — most notably **controlled foreign
corporation (CFC) rules**, which exist in some form in the US, most EU member states, and many
other countries, and can require a founder to report (and in some cases pay current-year tax
on) a foreign company's profits even before any dividend is paid out.¹⁷ Whether Odys's OÜ (or
whichever entity is chosen) would trigger CFC treatment, additional foreign-asset reporting, or
"place of effective management" tax-residency issues depends entirely on each founder's
specific country of tax residence, ownership percentage, and how the company is actually run
day to day — none of which this document assesses. **Before filing anything, engage a real
lawyer and accountant** — ideally one licensed in the chosen jurisdiction (Estonia, if that's
the final call) *and* one who understands each founder's home-country tax-residency
implications specifically. This is genuinely worth paying a professional for before acting, not
something to guess at from research like this.
